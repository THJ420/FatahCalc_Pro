How the Text ⟷ Cipher works

A cipher scrambles a message so only someone with the key can read it. The five here are classical ciphers — the ones used before computers, worked out with pencil and paper. They are wonderful for puzzles, escape rooms, geocaching and CTF challenges, and they are how cryptography is taught. None of them is secure today; a phone can break any of them in milliseconds.

Substitution, two ways

Every cipher on this page replaces letters rather than rearranging them. The difference is whether the replacement alphabet ever changes:

  • Caesar shift — slide the alphabet along by a fixed number. Julius Caesar reportedly used a shift of 3, so A→D. The key is just that number, which means there are only 25 useful keys.
  • ROT13 — a Caesar shift of exactly 13. Because 13 is half of 26, applying it twice returns the original text, so one operation both hides and reveals. Usenet used it to cover spoilers and punchlines.
  • Atbash — reverse the alphabet: A↔Z, B↔Y. It comes from Hebrew scribal tradition and appears in the Book of Jeremiah. It has no key at all, and it is also its own inverse.
  • Vigenère — a keyword sets a different shift for each position, repeating as it goes. With the key KEY, the first letter shifts by K (10), the second by E (4), the third by Y (24), then it starts over. This is polyalphabetic, and for three centuries it was called le chiffre indéchiffrable.
  • A1Z26 — not really encryption, just a substitution of numbers for letters: A=1 … Z=26. It is the classic first layer of a puzzle.

Why a Caesar shift falls instantly

Shifting the alphabet moves the letter frequencies but does not flatten them, so English fingerprints survive: E is still the most common letter, just wearing a disguise. Compare the letter counts of a candidate against known English frequencies with a chi-squared distance:

score = Σ (observed − expected)² ÷ expected expected = English frequency × total letters

The lowest score is almost always the right answer. That is exactly what the Solve without the key panel does — it tries all 26 shifts and ranks them, which is why a single-key cipher offers no real protection. Vigenère resists this, because each position uses a different alphabet and the frequencies genuinely do flatten out; it needs the longer Kasiski or Friedman analysis instead.

What is preserved

The letter ciphers keep your capitalisation, spaces, digits and punctuation exactly as typed and only move A–Z, so word shapes stay visible. Real cryptography deliberately destroys those clues — a reminder that these are puzzles, not protection. A1Z26 is the exception: it handles letters only and reports anything it had to drop.

Text ⟷ Cipher FAQ

Can I use these ciphers to protect sensitive data?

No. Classical ciphers are easy to break and offer no real security. Use modern encryption for anything confidential.

How does the Caesar cipher cracker work?

It tries all 26 shifts and ranks the results against English letter frequencies, then shows the most likely plaintext.
